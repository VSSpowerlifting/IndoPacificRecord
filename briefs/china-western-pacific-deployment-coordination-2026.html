Taiwan Reports More Than 100 Chinese Vessels Across the Western Pacific

Japanese patrols tracked one destroyer through repeated passages in the first island chain. The wider deployment raises questions about coordination across China's naval forces.

By Benjamin YangFounder & Executive Director

Introduction

A Chinese destroyer kept returning to the same passage south of Japan. On November 1, Japanese forces observed Qiqihar, hull 121, heading northwest between Okinawa and Miyako Island with a fast combat support ship. Early the next day it was moving southeast with a different oiler. On November 4, Japan found the destroyer headed northwest once more.

Deployment and coordination

At the same time, Taiwan was watching Chinese vessels spread across a much larger area. Taiwan News reported that National Security Council Secretary-General Joseph Wu said more than 100 navy and coast guard ships were operating across the Western Pacific as of November 3. Taiwan News reported that he accused Beijing of applying pressure and wrote, “We won’t provoke, but we won’t bow to any pressure.” That warning was about a regional deployment stretching far beyond the vessels Taiwan counts each morning around its own coast.

The striking feature of Wu’s account is the spread of forces. Reviewing the map, the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) identified four Chinese naval task forces from three military theater commands between Taiwan and Guam, including the Liaoning carrier group. It described a second concentration of navy and coast guard ships closer to Taiwan. ISW argued that this arrangement could be an unannounced rehearsal for isolating the island.

There is an operational logic to that interpretation. In a conflict, vessels operating near Taiwan could contest traffic approaching its ports and coast. Major combatants farther east would confront a different problem: forces or supplies arriving across the Pacific. Combining those roles would require China’s navy to link units operating far apart, potentially under different regional commands. Geography offers the outline of such an operation; coordination is what would make it work.

ISW said participation by the Eastern, Northern and Southern Theater Commands was unusual for Taiwan-focused exercises, which have generally centered on the Eastern command. That gives the assessment more weight than a ship count alone. Yet the map principally tells readers where formations were reported. It provides little public evidence of shared orders, synchronized assignments or joint execution. Separate deployments in the same week could produce a similar picture.

Beijing described the Liaoning group’s Western Pacific training in October as routine annual activity, while Taiwan called the broader deployment coercive. Neither statement settles the operational question. China could be conducting several training missions while gaining experience useful for a future isolation campaign; it could also be deliberately practicing how to bring them together. The evidence for those two explanations lies in how the ships operated, beyond their presence on a map.

Japan’s Joint Staff supplies an unusually clear sequence for one vessel. It linked Qiqihar and support ship hull 901 to an October 17 passage eastward through the Osumi Strait. The pair then moved northwest through the Okinawa–Miyako passage on November 1. Around 1 a.m. on November 2, Qiqihar headed southeast toward the Pacific alongside replenishment oiler hull 890. Japan reported the destroyer moving northwest through the passage again on November 4. Its P-1 and P-3C maritime patrol aircraft monitored the movements.

Those observations show the same warship crossing repeatedly between the East China Sea and the Pacific, with different support vessels alongside it. The changes in escort are a reason to examine logistics and task organization more closely. But Japan’s releases record passages, not an underway replenishment, a transfer of command or the destroyer’s assignment. There is no verified link between Qiqihar’s route and the four task forces ISW identified farther east.

The two reporting systems also answer different questions. Taiwan’s defense ministry recorded six navy ships and six other Chinese official vessels near Taiwan in the 24 hours ending November 3. Wu’s figure covered a far broader maritime area. Reuters had reported a previous regional map showing 53 Chinese navy and official ships on October 29. Those snapshots may indicate changing scale, but differing boundaries and counting periods prevent a reliable growth calculation. Japan’s hull-by-hull notices are more useful for reconstructing movement than estimating the entire fleet at sea.

Taiwan’s map, as interpreted by ISW, raises a credible question about forces operating in two strategically distinct zones. Japan independently documents repeated movement through an opening in the first island chain. Together they show regional distribution and access to the Pacific. They leave the relationship between the groups unresolved.

That relationship is the evidence to watch. A sustained pattern of task forces holding positions, aircraft and ships operating to a common timetable, or official records tying units from different commands to the same mission would strengthen the isolation-rehearsal hypothesis. Groups dispersing on unrelated schedules would support a looser explanation of overlapping training. For now, the central question is how the ships were being used together, rather than how often they appeared in the daily count.

Benjamin Yang

Founder & Executive Director

Benjamin Yang studies International Affairs at George Washington University's Elliott School, with a focus on Indo-Pacific security, U.S.–China relations, and regional strategic affairs. He founded Indo-Pacific Record to make official defense and security publications more accessible and to examine what those sources reveal—and leave unresolved—about regional developments. As Executive Director, he leads the publication's research and editorial direction, organizational operations, and platform development, emphasizing primary-source evidence, transparent documentation, and careful distinctions between official claims and independent analysis.
