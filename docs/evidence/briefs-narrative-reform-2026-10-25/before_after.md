## After — what Dylan now receives (editable region, article first)

```text
## WORKING TITLE
A navy says its new towed sonar heard a submarine 11 kilometres away


## DEK
The Tavaran Navy reports that a domestically built sonar array tripled its detection range in shallow water; the claim rests on the navy's own account.


## ARTICLE
The Tavaran Navy has spent three weeks towing a new sonar array behind the patrol ship Marisol in the shallow Kesh Strait, and on 14 October it said what the array found. Rear Admiral Ilan Voss, the navy's chief, told reporters that the system picked up a diesel-electric submarine from 11 kilometres away in water about 60 metres deep.

A towed array is a long cable of underwater microphones pulled behind a ship, far enough back to escape the ship's own engine noise. The navy did not name the submarine, say who operated it, or release any recordings.

**Why shallow water is hard**

Sonar works best in deep, cold ocean, where sound travels in long, predictable curves. Over a seabed only 60 metres down, the same pulse bounces between the surface and the bottom, and a quiet submarine blends into the echoes. Navies that operate near coasts have long treated this as the hardest version of the detection problem.

That is why the claimed range matters more than the ship or the array. Voss said the sonar previously fitted to the Marisol class managed about four kilometres in the same strait, so the navy is reporting a gain of nearly threefold. Both figures come from the navy, and it gave no test conditions for either.

The design follows from the physics. A sonar fixed to the hull has to listen through the ship's own noise, so trailing the array behind the stern removes most of it. The price is manoeuvrability: a ship dragging a cable that long turns slowly and cannot easily back up.

**A domestic design**

The array was built by the Tavara Naval Research Institute, which Voss said began work in 2023 after a request for imported systems drew no bid the navy could afford. Production will start next year at the Port Sellis yard, with the first sets going to the four ships of the Marisol class.

But the announcement contains no budget and no delivery schedule beyond next year. Voss said the cost per ship was "within existing allocations", which leaves open whether the navy is buying four arrays or committing to a longer production run.

For a navy of this size the practical question is how many ships get the system, not whether one trial worked. Voss said the array fits the winches already installed on all four ships, so each refit should need only a few days in port.

**What would settle it**

The next evidence will be practical rather than rhetorical. If the navy publishes the test conditions, or invites an outside observer to the autumn exercise it has scheduled in the strait, the 11-kilometre figure can be judged against the seabed and sea state that produced it.

Until then the claim is a navy statement about its own equipment, and a plausible one: the gain it describes is large, but not larger than a new array tuned to one strait could deliver.


## EDITORIAL QUESTIONS / SATURDAY FOLLOW-UP
Please check the 11 km and 4 km figures and the 'within existing allocations' quotation against the original wording before anything is public.
```

Checks on the "after" text: none — no mechanical errors and no editorial flags (headline: *Narrative QA: 0 mechanical error(s) (0 major), 0 editorial flag(s); no mechanical hold*).
