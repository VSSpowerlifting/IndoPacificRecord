# Indo-Pacific Record Briefs — Editorial Voice & Style Guide

**Version:** 1.0 — 28 October 2026  
**Owner:** Founder & Director of Research and Editorial  
**Status:** Editorial direction approved; implementation in the automated Sunday writer is a separate, reviewable change.  
**Applies to:** Initial machine-assisted Sunday manuscripts, their human editing, and new IPR Briefs when the same voice is appropriate. It does not retroactively rewrite published Briefs or govern the model-labeled Daily record.

> **Report first. Explain second. Interpret carefully. Stop when the point is made.**

## 1. The voice

Indo-Pacific Record should sound like a specialist publication whose reporters know the institutions and have read the underlying documents. It is **investigative, analytically confident, restrained, and readable**. Journalism supplies the opening and movement; research discipline supplies the evidence, attribution and limits.

The aim is **more journalistic than academic, without losing analytical precision**. Readers should come away understanding one concrete development and the particular observation IPR can justify about it. A Brief is neither a mechanical recap of official announcements nor an essay in which a generic regional-security thesis precedes the evidence.

Write for policy professionals and analysts first, but allow an informed nonspecialist to understand every necessary term. Prefer expert substance to jargon. The preferred tendency is **reporting and explanation before interpretation**—roughly a 70/30 editorial feel when the story supports it, never a numerical quota.

**Do not aim to evade AI detection.** The objective is distinctive, readable, original and factually accountable prose, regardless of how the first draft was produced. A natural rhythm must never come at the expense of a necessary caveat.

## 2. Five failure modes to correct

| Common tendency | Why it fails | Editorial correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Repetitive qualification** | Every paragraph sounds afraid of its own findings. | State the uncertainty precisely, once at the claim it changes. Repeat only if a different claim genuinely needs the same qualification. |
| **Predictable paragraph construction** | Fact → caveat → interpretation → caveat becomes a visible machine rhythm. | Let paragraph order follow the story: an action, a mechanism, an example, an official contrast, a consequence, or a gap. Some paragraphs should simply report. |
| **Research-process narration** | The article comments on its own source evaluation instead of telling readers what happened. | Name the issuer or document when relevant, then state the reported fact. Put internal checking instructions and inconsequential methodological notes in the private audit. |
| **Excessive abstraction** | Institutional and strategic language obscures ships, people, locations, quantities and decisions. | Replace broad nouns with named actors, verbs, places and observable actions whenever the evidence permits. Explain the implications after the fact is clear. |
| **Uniform structure** | Every story feels like the same worksheet with different country names. | Choose the sequence, headings and ending for this story. Do not force a regional comparison, “why it matters” section or summary conclusion. |

These are diagnoses, **not forbidden grammatical constructions**. One well-placed qualification, abstract term or contrast may be exactly right. Edit repetition and imprecision, not vocabulary by counting tokens.

## 3. Before the first sentence: find the angle

A selected topic is not yet a story. Before drafting, write an internal one-sentence editorial angle that answers:

1. **What happened?** Identify the actor, action, place and relevant event date or period.
2. **What is the revealing detail?** Find the operation, quantity, wording difference, implementation step, sequence or practical mechanism that makes this worth reading.
3. **What is the actual baseline?** Compare with documented earlier practice only when it is available. Unknown is not “first,” “new,” “escalating” or “unprecedented.”
4. **What can IPR add?** Explain context, a comparison, an institutional distinction or a limitation the original announcement leaves unclear.
5. **What cannot be claimed?** Record the decisive uncertainty, provenance restriction and any translation issue before writing.

Choose **one coherent development or tightly related theme**. A single-country article is complete on its own. Multiple desks should enter only when their verified accounts bear on the same issue. Do not give countries airtime to satisfy a quota or invent a link because events happened in the same week.

The angle is private drafting guidance, not a claim that the story has been approved.

## 4. The lead: concrete before conceptual

Open with the action or revealing observation—not a sweeping geopolitical premise and usually not the research workflow. In the opening paragraph, give the reader the essential **who, what, where and when** and a reason to continue. Attribution may appear immediately when the statement is a disputed claim or the report itself is the news.

**Prefer:**
- “During the exercise, a boarding team from one navy trained aboard another country's patrol ship.”
- “The ministry described the deployment as a scheduled rotation, but its published dates differ from the exercise calendar.”

**Avoid as routine openers:**
- “Amid rising tensions across the Indo-Pacific…”
- “A review of the available official record reveals…”
- “In an increasingly complex regional security landscape…”

Do not bury the concrete event beneath a source count, the week-ending date, the existence of a press release or a claim that “the record shows.” If the source's **publication** is itself the development—such as release of a previously unavailable policy document—it is appropriate to lead with that publication.

Never fill a dramatic lead by inventing weather, scenes, personal experience, conversations, operational details or eyewitness observations.

## 5. Narrative progression, not a section formula

Each paragraph should perform a distinct job and make the next paragraph necessary. Possible jobs include establishing the scene from documented facts, explaining a mechanism, introducing the baseline, showing a difference between accounts, separating intention from execution, describing a constraint, or identifying the observable follow-up.

Choose the shape that fits the evidence:

- **Chronology** when the sequence changes interpretation.
- **Mechanism** when readers need to understand how an exercise, system or policy works.
- **Comparison** when two supported accounts illuminate one issue.
- **Implementation** when the gap between an announcement and actual activity is the story.
- **Evidence tension** when the most interesting finding is a verifiable discrepancy.

These are editorial possibilities, **not required sections**. Use short, story-specific subheads only where they aid navigation. Avoid boilerplate headings such as “Overview,” “What Stood Out,” “Why It Matters,” “Across the Region” and “Conclusion.” A no-subhead article is acceptable when it reads better.

Do not invent extra analytical paragraphs merely to hit a word target. Follow the active writer's technical length and block limits, but value purposeful development over padding.

## 6. Sentence and paragraph craft

Write with named subjects and active verbs where accurate: *deployed, hosted, trained, announced, ordered, funded, reported, amended, exercised*. Keep the distinction between **said it would happen** and **did happen** in the verb.

Use technical language when it contributes precision, then define it once in ordinary English. Expand unfamiliar acronyms on first use; do not assume the reader knows the organizational chart of every military or ministry. Retain exact official terms where translation matters and record uncertainties for source review.

Let sentence length reflect meaning. A complex explanation can be long. A concrete finding may need six words. Do not engineer arbitrary variation for its own sake. Avoid a stream of perfectly symmetrical sentences, neat three-item abstractions or identical paragraph lengths.

Transitional language should connect real reasoning, not decorate it. Use “but,” “because,” “however” and “meanwhile” when there is an actual contrast, cause or simultaneous development. Omit connective phrases when one sentence naturally follows another.

Use em dashes, colons, rhetorical questions and “not X but Y” contrasts sparingly. They are useful devices, not a recognizable house rhythm. Avoid frequent “not merely X, but Y,” “underscores,” “highlights the importance,” “signals a shift,” “evolving landscape,” “pivotal,” “testament to,” “taken together” and “time will tell.” Do **not** impose blanket word bans; judge whether a phrase is informative in context.

## 7. Claims, attribution and confidence

The voice can be confident **because its claims are narrow and supported**. Keep these four levels distinct:

| Claim type | Treatment in published prose |
| --- | --- |
| **Documented event or action** | State directly with an appropriate source trail and dates. Verify event time separately from announcement and publication time. |
| **Issuer's assertion** | Attribute to the named institution, particularly for readiness, performance, costs, motives and operational results. Never turn its assertion into independent confirmation. |
| **IPR's inference or comparison** | Explain the concrete premise and what inference follows. Use language proportionate to the evidence; identify IPR's judgment when needed. |
| **Unknown or disputed matter** | State the uncertainty where it materially changes the central reading. Do not imply that missing collected evidence proves an institution was silent or an activity never occurred. |

An issuer may **announce**, **describe**, **claim**, **report** or **attribute**. Those verbs are not interchangeable. “Completed,” “deployed,” “delivered,” “tested,” “plans” and “intends” must reflect the actual source status.

An official outlet's messaging does not establish the government's undisclosed intent. Parallel events, similar language or shared timing do not by themselves establish coordination, causation, escalation or operational interoperability.

Do not invent quotations, translation equivalents, proper names, figures, source independence, dates, prior baselines or technical explanations. A fluent sentence with unsupported substance is a failure, even if its style is excellent.

## 8. Uncertainty belongs where it changes the story

**Keep a necessary qualification. Move, consolidate or cut repetitive qualification.**

A caveat belongs in the public article when it changes the reader's interpretation of a central figure, action, chronology or strategic conclusion. State it close to the affected claim, preferably in plain and specific language. Example: “Both range figures came from the navy; it did not publish comparable test conditions.”

A general explanation of the archive's limits, an unused source, a translation term to inspect or the author's unsuccessful search usually belongs in the **private editorial audit**, not in the reader-facing prose. Exceptions are important source limitations that directly govern the argument and cannot be disclosed merely in sitewide methodology.

Replace broad formulas such as “the available record does not establish” with the **particular missing evidence** when that omission matters: *No budget was provided; neither ministry specified a delivery date; the account gives no exercise outcome.* Do not omit an important unknown just to produce a decisive-sounding ending.

A weak claim plus many disclaimers is not an analytical accomplishment. Often the better edit is to **narrow or remove the claim**.

## 9. Titles, deks, headings and endings

**Title:** Name the action and its specific tension or significance. Favor informative verbs and concrete nouns over vague “regional implications,” inflated novelty or a punchy claim the article cannot prove.

**Dek:** Add a new fact, distinction or important qualification; do not repeat the title in longer words. Usually one or two sentences.

**Subheads:** Describe what the following section actually does. They are optional and should not be identical from one Brief to the next.

**Ending:** Finish with a concrete consequence, documented open question, meaningful difference between accounts or specific indicator to watch. Do not automatically recap the introduction. Avoid ending every paragraph—and especially every article—with “more evidence is needed.” Sometimes the most honest ending is a precise finding that is already established.

## 10. Four practical before-and-after examples

**All examples below are invented teaching material.** “Tavara,” its navy, ships, people, dates and figures are fictitious. They are not source evidence or assertions about an actual government.

### A. Source-first opening → event-first opening

**Instead of:** “A review of the available record indicates that the Tavaran Navy published a statement about a towed sonar system. This development underscores the importance of maritime awareness.”

**Write toward:** “The Tavaran Navy says a towed sonar array detected a submarine 11 kilometres from its patrol ship during trials in shallow water. The claimed range matters because the navy says its previous system reached only four kilometres in the same strait.”

**Why:** The action and comparison arrive before the author's research process. The performance remains explicitly attributed.

### B. Ritual caveats → one useful limitation

**Instead of:** “The available record suggests a substantial improvement. This could not be independently verified. It is important to note that the record does not establish whether the test conditions were comparable.”

**Write toward:** “The navy reported a nearly threefold increase in range, but did not give the test conditions needed to compare the two figures.”

**Why:** The reader sees exactly what remains unknown without hearing three versions of the same disclaimer.

### C. Abstract significance → an operational explanation

**Instead of:** “The development reflects a broader effort to strengthen complex maritime-security cooperation and demonstrates the importance of enhanced interoperability.”

**Write toward:** “The visiting team used the host ship's boats and procedures during the boarding exercise. That required the sailors to work through equipment and command routines together, although the account gave no performance measure.”

**Why:** Specific activity explains the relevant form of cooperation. It does not claim that interoperability was independently demonstrated.

### D. Generic conclusion → a grounded ending

**Instead of:** “Ultimately, these developments highlight the evolving nature of regional security and the need for continued monitoring.”

**Write toward:** “The navy has announced plans to fit the array to four patrol ships. Its next procurement notice will show whether the trial is becoming a fleet-wide program.”

**Why:** The last sentence identifies a real next test of the announced policy, conditional on source verification.

## 11. The Sunday writer's positive instructions

When a first draft is machine-assisted, apply this guide **during first-pass generation**, not as an after-the-fact “make it human” rewrite. The writer should:

1. Select one supported angle before drafting; preserve the existing story-selection and abstention rules.
2. Lead with the concrete development, with immediately necessary attribution.
3. Build a single connected article whose paragraphs each add a new fact, mechanism, comparison or inference.
4. Use concrete details that **actually appear in permitted evidence**; never fabricate a scene, extra context or technical explanation.
5. Explain terms in clear language, distinguishing direct evidence from IPR interpretation.
6. State each material limitation at the point of use and keep editorial process notes in private audit fields.
7. Vary syntax naturally, avoid stock significance claims, and use headings only when useful.
8. End on an earned finding or observable next indicator, not a generic geopolitical summary.
9. Maintain every required citation/provenance mapping, original-language verification boundary and source-use restriction.
10. Abstain, narrow the thesis or escalate to human review if the evidence cannot sustain the desired article.

**Compact prompt passage for later integration (not currently runtime-active):**

~~~text
IPR EDITORIAL VOICE. Write as a source-grounded foreign-policy journalist with
analyst discipline: investigative, analytically confident, restrained and
readable. Report first. Explain second. Interpret carefully. Stop when the
point is made. Build one article around the most concrete verified
development. Lead with named actors and observable action, not the existence
of your sources or broad regional stakes. Explain the mechanism or baseline
that makes the news meaningful. Every paragraph must advance the story;
paragraphs need not repeat a fact/caveat/interpretation pattern. Prefer
specific verbs, places, figures and institutional roles over abstract
significance language. Give a technical term one accurate plain-English
explanation. Attribute official claims and distinguish intention,
announcement, implementation and demonstrated result. Keep each material
uncertainty near the claim it limits, once when possible; never suppress an
essential caveat. Keep source-processing commentary and unresolved editorial
checks in private audit fields. Use only story-specific optional headings.
End on a concrete finding or verifiable next indicator. Do not fabricate
scenes, context, comparisons, intent, figures, quotations or supporting
evidence. If support is insufficient, say less or abstain.
~~~

This passage **supplements**, and never replaces, the live evidence, strict-tool schema, provenance, source-rights, cost/retry, human-review and publication controls.

## 12. The editor's six-question read

Before forwarding to an editor or considering approval, read the article **without** the private audit and ask:

1. **Can I identify the event within two sentences?** If not, move the action to the front.
2. **Does every paragraph add something?** Cut a paragraph that only rephrases the previous one.
3. **Could I replace these institutions with any other countries and keep the same article?** If yes, make the reporting more specific.
4. **Are the caveats both sufficient and economical?** Keep the decisive ones; cut repetitions and internal housekeeping.
5. **Does the interpretation follow from these particular sources?** If not, narrow the claim or make it a research question.
6. **Is the ending earned?** Stop before the generic recap.

These are **human editorial questions, not an AI-likeness score**. Style alone must never approve or reject source integrity.

## 13. Applying the guide without a second writing pipeline

The current intended order is:

**Eligible verified weekly evidence → story selection/abstention → first-pass article using this voice → existing offline structure/provenance checks → human language edit → claim-by-claim reconciliation → owner approval and governed publication.**

- The Sunday writer's planned article-first contract and offline prose QA are in **PR #880**. That PR is **unmerged at the time this guide is written**. Its own technical document, **docs/IPR_BRIEFS_WRITING_STANDARD.md**, governs field shapes, block limits, private source accounting, warning/hold behavior and editor returns once released. This guide defines the editorial voice; it does not pretend those mechanisms are live today.
- Treat mechanical defects and unsupported claims as reasons for correction/hold under existing rules. Treat clichés, caveat density and rhythmic sameness as **advisory editorial flags**; false positives are expected.
- Do not add automatic “humanizer” passes, model retries, a second prose engine or higher token budgets merely to chase style. First compare a real owner-only draft against this guide.
- Where available, compare the original first pass and Dylan's edited return offline. A repeated preference becomes a candidate for a prompt change only after the owner approves it and corresponding regression checks are added.
- Any implementation update must preserve source-to-block citations, verification against original-language sources, source permission boundaries and human editorial release. Dylan's structural edits do not themselves verify facts. Reconcile altered claims before approval.
- Do not send, spend, merge, number, publish or deploy merely because this guide exists.

## 14. Success criteria

Compare one current first-pass manuscript with the previous drafting style using **the same eligible evidence** when feasible. Judge both for:

- **Specificity:** identifiable actors, actions and meaningful detail in the lead.
- **Narrative momentum:** paragraphs advance one story without worksheet repetition.
- **Analytical payoff:** a defensible, non-obvious observation rather than generic importance.
- **Attribution and limits:** no weaker sourcing, hidden uncertainty or inflated claims.
- **Readability:** fewer redundant caveats and research-process sentences; natural sentence movement.
- **Editorial effort:** fewer substantive language/structure interventions from Dylan or the owner.

Do not manufacture a quantitative quality score from regex counts. Record examples of what improved, what got worse and any material factual risk. **A style improvement that makes a claim less supportable is not a success.**
