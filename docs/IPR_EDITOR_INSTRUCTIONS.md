# IPR Editor Instructions

*Indo-Pacific Record — standing instructions for every manuscript edit.
Version of 1 November 2026. These instructions travel with every manuscript;
the story-specific details arrive separately in that manuscript's assignment.*

Indo-Pacific Record (IPR) is an independent, source-grounded research
publication on Indo-Pacific defence and security. Each IPR Brief reports one
development from official records and explains why it matters. You have been
asked to edit a Brief before publication. This document explains what you
receive, what is yours to change, what is not, and how to send it back.

## 1. Who does what

- **Ben (Founder; final editorial approval)** owns accuracy, the argument and
  publication. Nothing is published without his explicit approval of the
  final text.
- **You, the assigned editor,** own language and structure: clarity, order,
  headings, length and flow. Each manuscript has exactly one assigned editor.
- **Drafting tools and automated checks approve nothing.** A manuscript may
  have been drafted with machine assistance; treat every sentence as a draft
  that a careful human still has to make good.

You will receive two things: these standing instructions, and a short
**assignment** for the specific manuscript (story, due time, questions for
you, and anything not to change). Where the assignment is more specific, it
governs that manuscript.

## 2. What you receive

- **The Word file** — your editing surface. It opens with the title (Title
  style) and the dek, a one-sentence summary beneath it (Subtitle style),
  followed by the article. At the end, a heading reads **Source links for
  context**; everything below it is reference only.
- **A text worksheet (TXT)**, when supplied — the full record of the draft,
  including any editorial questions from the drafting process and a private
  source appendix. Read it for context. You do not need to edit it.

## 3. Voice

IPR's governing instruction is: **Report first. Explain second. Interpret
carefully. Stop when the point is made.**

Write for an informed reader who has not followed the story: a policy
staffer, a journalist, a student of the region. The register is a careful
foreign-policy journalist with an analyst's discipline — natural, varied
sentences; the claim, then its basis; technical terms explained once, in plain
English, where the reader first meets them; acronyms spelled out on first use.

Cut stock significance language ("underscores", "increasingly complex
landscape"), throat-clearing about sources, recaps of the lead and endings
that only restate. A paragraph may report a fact and stop.

Six questions to ask of the draft:

1. Can the reader identify the event within the first two sentences?
2. Does every paragraph add something — a fact, a mechanism, a real
   comparison or a defensible inference?
3. Could the institutions be swapped without changing the text? If so, it is
   too generic.
4. Are the caveats sufficient, and stated once, where they matter?
5. Does the interpretation follow from these sources, and no further?
6. Is the ending earned?

## 4. Length and scope

A Brief ordinarily runs **700–1,200 words**; around 900–1,050 is a good
target, not a requirement. Cut anything that does not earn its place. If the
draft is short because the evidence is thin, do not pad it — say so in your
note. If the story needs substantially more than 1,200 words to be told
properly, say that too; it may belong in a longer format.

Each Brief covers **one coherent development**, or a closely connected set of
them. If you think the draft has bundled unrelated stories, flag it rather
than restructuring it into two pieces.

## 5. Your editorial freedom

These are yours to change, without asking first:

- wording, sentence order, paragraph order and paragraph breaks;
- subheadings — rename, add, remove or reorder them (use a Heading style);
- cuts, merges and tightening;
- the opening and the ending;
- the wording of the title and dek — keep both in place, at the top, in the
  Title and Subtitle styles.

Strong structural edits are welcome. If the draft buries the development,
lead with it.

## 6. Factual boundaries

These protect the publication's credibility. Please hold to them strictly.

- **Add no facts that are not already in the draft.** No new figures, dates,
  names, titles, quotations or events — even ones you are confident of. If
  something important is missing or looks wrong, raise it in a comment; Ben
  will check it against the sources.
- **Do not change what a source said.** Keep quotations word for word, keep
  each statement attributed to whoever made it, and keep the attribution's
  strength ("said", "claimed", "according to").
- **Do not turn a claim into a fact.** An official statement, allegation or
  framing stays attributed to its source, however plausible it is.
- **Keep necessary caveats.** You may move or tighten a qualification, but do
  not remove one the reader needs (for example, that a figure is the issuer's
  own).
- **Do not strengthen the interpretation** beyond what the draft supports. If
  you think the analysis can go further, propose it in a comment.

When in doubt, comment rather than edit.

## 7. Citations and sources

- Do not add, invent, remove or move source links or citations.
- Leave the **Source links for context** heading and everything below it
  untouched, and add nothing after it.
- Never paste source identifiers, record IDs or worksheet markers into the
  article.
- Keep the article as plain paragraphs and headings: no tables, text boxes,
  images or footnotes. Text placed in them can be lost on intake.

## 8. Track Changes and comments

1. Turn on **Track Changes** before you start.
2. Edit directly in the text. Use **comments** for questions, suggestions you
   are unsure about, facts to check and anything you would not change
   yourself.
3. **Do not accept or reject your own changes.** Return the file with every
   change still tracked. Ben reviews each change, accepts or rejects it, and
   only then takes the text forward.

If you answer an editorial question from the TXT worksheet, do so in a
comment or in your reply note — not in the article.

## 9. Turnaround

Your assignment gives the due date and time (Eastern Time). Unless it says
otherwise, allow about **48 hours** from receipt; for the regular Sunday
manuscript, the target is **Monday, 8 p.m. ET**. Early returns are welcome. If
you cannot make the deadline, or cannot take the assignment at all, tell Ben
as early as you can. A late edit is still useful; a silent one is not.

## 10. How to return the manuscript

Reply **in the same email thread** you received the manuscript in, so the
subject line ("IPR Brief edit — …") stays intact, and attach:

- the edited **Word file**, with changes still tracked (keep the original
  file name, adding your initials if you like); and
- a short **note** in the email: the main changes you made and why, any open
  questions or facts to check, and anything you think should not run.

If you edited the TXT worksheet instead of the Word file, change only the
prose under the manuscript headings; leave every heading, any source-ID line
and the whole source appendix exactly as received.

## 11. Credit

Bylines and credit are decided issue by issue by Ben, based on each
contributor's role in that Brief. Editing a manuscript, or appearing on IPR's
staff page, does not by itself carry a byline. If you believe your
contribution warrants credit, say so in your reply.

## 12. Confidentiality

The manuscript is an **unapproved, unpublished draft**. Do not share,
forward, quote or post it, or paste it into outside tools or services,
without Ben's permission. Delete local copies once the Brief is published or
withdrawn, unless Ben asks you to keep them.

## 13. Human-drafted manuscripts

Everything above applies equally when the draft was written by a person
rather than with machine assistance, including Ben. The standard is the same.
