China–Singapore Naval Cooperation

What official Chinese and Singaporean publications establish about the two navies’ cooperation since 2015, and where that record stops.

Research question How is China–Singapore naval cooperation evolving, and what does the public record establish about its scope, depth, and limits?

Official Chinese and Singaporean publications show a regular, institutionalised and modest naval relationship. The bilateral exercise Maritime Cooperation has been held five times: once in 2015 and every year since 2023, with the host alternating between Singapore and Zhanjiang. It sits within a 2008 defence agreement, enhanced in 2019 with commitments to regular and larger bilateral exercises and to visiting-forces and logistics arrangements. Each edition brought one or two ships from each navy for a few days of planning, professional exchange and sea serials such as gunnery, communications and manoeuvring, search and rescue and medical evacuation, with mine warfare and helicopter deck landings in some years. Around the exercise sit a routine PLA Navy technical stop at Changi, multinational cadet events and ministerial meetings. China describes the cooperation as deepening; Singapore places it among many naval partnerships. The record is official self-description. It documents regularity, institutional commitments and the activities each government chose to report; it does not measure interoperability, readiness or strategic alignment.

Five editions, one interrupted series

The core of the public record is a single bilateral naval exercise, Maritime Cooperation. Official releases document five editions. Counting them from 2015 hides an eight-year gap.

01

2015: the inaugural edition in Singapore

Link

MINDEF reported the inaugural edition in Singapore on May 20 and May 23–25, 2015. Singapore sent RSS Intrepid and RSS Valiant; the PLA Navy sent the frigate Yulin. MINDEF listed gunnery and manoeuvring serials at sea.

Qualification. This is Singapore’s official account. It is the only 2015 publication reviewed here; no Chinese account of the first edition was checked.

03

2023: the second edition, eight years later

Link

MINDEF announced the second edition for April 28 to May 1, 2023, eight years after the first, opening at Changi Naval Base. Singapore sent the frigate RSS Intrepid and the mine countermeasures vessel RSS Punggol; the PLA Navy sent the frigate Yulin and the minesweeper Chibi. The planned sea phase in the southern South China Sea included helicopter cross-deck landings, gunnery, replenishment-at-sea approaches, search and rescue, simulated minefield transits, and communications and manoeuvring drills.

Qualification. The release was issued on the opening day and describes planned activity. This dossier did not review a closing release or a Chinese account of the 2023 edition.

08

Hosts have alternated since 2023

Link

Since 2023 the host has alternated each year: Singapore in 2023, Zhanjiang in 2024, Singapore in 2025 and Zhanjiang in 2026. Before the 2024 edition, a Xinhua report published on China’s defence ministry website stated an aim of a regular mechanism with hosts alternating annually.

Qualification. Four years make a pattern, not a commitment. The Chinese report stated an aim; this dossier found no published bilateral agreement that fixes the arrangement, and no Singaporean statement of the same aim was reviewed.

Activities and participation

Each edition paired one or two ships from each navy with planning ashore and a set of basic sea serials. The two governments often describe the same serials in different words, and a plan announced before an exercise is not a record of what was done.

09

China’s 2024 plan listed boarding

Link

Before the 2024 edition, a Xinhua report published on China’s defence ministry website said the exercise was held under the annual plan and by mutual consensus. It listed a joint surface strike, replenishment-at-sea positioning, joint search and rescue, and visit, board, search and seizure among the planned sea serials.

Qualification. This is a pre-exercise plan. It does not say which navy would conduct each serial; Singapore’s account of its ship’s role follows.

China called 2024 longer and deeper

Link

As the 2024 edition opened, a Xinhua report published on China’s defence ministry website said that, compared with the two earlier editions, it ran longer, had more serials and involved deeper exchange. It said the two navies were building the exercise series into a signature programme.

12

2026: the same serials in two vocabularies

Link

The two concluding accounts describe largely the same activity in different terms. China’s defence ministry lists live firing, formation movement, casualty transfer and treatment, and an exchange of observers; MINDEF lists gunnery firings, manoeuvring, medical evacuation and an exchange of sea riders. Both list communications drills and joint search and rescue, with planning ashore, which China’s account calls a tabletop exercise. Only the Chinese accounts list replenishment at sea, ship tours and a deck reception. PLA Daily’s opening report also listed a joint surface strike among the planned serials; neither concluding account names one.

Qualification. The pairing of terms is IPR’s reading; neither government defines its terms. Both lists are official summaries, and their silence on the planned strike serial does not show that it was dropped.

13

Singapore has sent one or two ships to each edition

Link

Across the five editions Singapore sent one or two ships: two in 2015, 2023 and 2025, and one frigate in 2024 and 2026. The PLA Navy’s named contribution was one ship in 2015, two in 2023, 2024 and 2025, and one frigate in 2026. The two-ship editions added mine warfare vessels, and in 2024 China also listed shipborne helicopters and a marine detachment. Announced windows ran from four days in 2023 to eight in 2025. The comparable counts establish that Singapore’s ship commitment has stayed at one or two vessels; they do not show a rise in either navy’s ships, although China’s 2024 pre-exercise report stated an aim of expanding the exercise’s scale. The listed serials vary by edition rather than growing steadily.

Qualification. Ship numbers are one measure. Capability mix, duration and serials changed from edition to edition, personnel numbers are not consistently published, and no reviewed source measures the complexity of a serial. Durations are announced windows. A steady ship count shows neither stagnation nor deepening.

Port calls, exchanges and meetings

Beyond the exercise, the record shows logistical port use, multilateral professional events and ministerial meetings. Each is narrower than its headline suggests.

Qualification. MINDEF presents the stop as routine and in line with its practice for many navies. Its release does not give arrival or departure dates.

Agreements and mechanisms

MINDEF traces the first exercise to a Four-Point Consensus of November 2014. The main published framework is a 2008 defence agreement, enhanced in 2019. The record shows its exercise commitment in practice more clearly than its other naval-relevant provisions.

18

2019: an enhanced defence agreement

Link

A MINDEF factsheet records that defence ministers Ng Eng Hen and Wei Fenghe signed an enhanced Agreement on Defence Exchanges and Security Cooperation on October 20, 2019, updating the 2008 original. Three of its new areas bear on naval activity: regularising and scaling up bilateral exercises across the army, navy and air force; a Visiting Forces Agreement for troops in bilateral exercises; and a mutual logistics support arrangement. The others are a regular ministerial dialogue, academic exchanges and a bilateral hotline.

Qualification. The factsheet lists commitments, not their execution.

What the record establishes

28

Regular and institutionalised, with modest demonstrated depth

Link

China–Singapore naval cooperation has become regular. After a single edition in 2015 and an eight-year gap, Maritime Cooperation has been held every year since 2023, with the host alternating between Singapore and Zhanjiang. It rests on published institutional arrangements: a 2008 defence agreement enhanced in 2019 with commitments to regular and larger exercises, visiting-forces and logistics arrangements and a regular ministerial dialogue, and the two defence ministers met again in 2026. Its reported content is consistent across editions: one or two ships a side, a few days of planning and professional exchange ashore, and sea serials in gunnery, communications and manoeuvring, search and rescue and medical evacuation, with mine warfare and helicopter deck landings in some years. Around it, a PLA Navy task force made a routine technical stop at Changi and Singaporean cadets joined a PLA Navy multinational camp. Ship numbers have stayed small, at one or two a side. Beyond this the published record is limited, not negative. A joint surface strike listed among the planned 2026 serials appears in neither reviewed concluding account, and that omission does not establish whether it occurred. No publication reviewed reports the visiting-forces or logistics arrangements in use. Limited published evidence of outcomes is not evidence that the cooperation lacks depth. The record supports describing an established and regular naval exercise relationship, small in the ships committed, framed by China as deepening and by Singapore as one of many naval partnerships.

Qualification. This is IPR’s synthesis of official publications. It describes what was published, not what was achieved, and would change with closing accounts of earlier editions or evidence of the agreement’s other provisions in use.

Scope and limitations

Research question. How is China–Singapore naval cooperation evolving, and what does the public record establish about its scope, depth, and limits?

Thirteen publications preserved in IPR’s record from July–October 2026 and nine linked official pages from 2015–2025. The preserved records are PLA Daily reports, China defence ministry statements, MINDEF releases and one Xinhua report carried by PLA Daily. The linked pages are seven MINDEF publications, including a parliamentary reply and a factsheet, and two Xinhua reports published on China’s defence ministry website.

Outside this scope: Unpublished activity, media and think-tank commentary, coast guard and non-naval cooperation, photographs and any claim to an exhaustive survey of either government’s reporting.

Compare dated official accounts edition by edition. Event dates stay distinct from publication dates, and plans stay distinct from reported execution. Chinese evidence is read in the original. Linked pages outside the record are cited by publisher, date and link only.

The preserved record covers July to October 2026. Earlier editions rely on linked official pages checked on October 26, 2026 and not preserved by IPR. No paid model processing was used. Restricted source text is not reproduced.
