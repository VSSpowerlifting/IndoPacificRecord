China–Singapore Naval Cooperation

What official Chinese and Singaporean publications establish about the two navies’ cooperation since 2015, what each government reports was actually done, and where that record stops.

Research question How is China–Singapore naval cooperation evolving, and what does the public record establish about its scope, depth, and limits?

Official Chinese and Singaporean publications show a regular, institutionalised and modest naval relationship. The bilateral exercise Maritime Cooperation has been held five times: once in 2015 and every year since 2023, with the host alternating between Singapore and Zhanjiang. Singapore reports a pandemic pause in flagship exercises, which fits the absence of an edition planned for 2020. Each edition brought one or two ships from each navy for a few days of planning, professional exchange and sea serials. Closing accounts report gunnery for every edition, usually with communications and manoeuvring and search and rescue, and medical evacuation, mine-warfare transits and replenishment approaches in some years. Advance plans listed more: Chinese reports included a joint surface strike for 2024, 2025 and 2026 and boarding serials for 2024 and 2025, and no closing account reviewed names either. The exercise sits within a 2008 defence agreement, enhanced in 2019. Of its naval-relevant commitments, regular exercising and a ministerial dialogue are documented in use; a secure defence telephone link reached a signed memorandum in 2023 with no reported use; and no instrument was found for the visiting-forces or logistics arrangements. China describes the cooperation as deepening; Singapore places it among many naval partnerships. The record is official self-description. It does not measure interoperability, readiness or strategic alignment.

Five editions compared

Each edition below gives the host, the ships, the dates and the activities that a closing or post-exercise account reports as done. What was announced in advance follows in the next section. Counting the editions from 2015 hides an eight-year gap.

Source: MINDEF’s release on the final day, reporting the exercise concluded.

06

2020: a planned edition and the pandemic pause

Link

A May 2019 MINDEF release said the two defence ministers had agreed to hold the next edition in 2020. None of the publications reviewed records a 2020 edition. MINDEF later said that flagship bilateral exercises had been put on hold by the COVID-19 pandemic, in June 2022 and again in a 2023 parliamentary reply, and its 2023 closing release quoted the Singaporean commander calling that edition a resumption after the pandemic restrictions.

Qualification. Both statements describe a pause in exercises generally. Neither says in terms that a 2020 edition of Maritime Cooperation was cancelled.

Advance plans and closing accounts

Releases issued before or on an exercise’s first day describe plans. Closing and post-exercise accounts describe what each government says was done. The two lists often differ. A planned serial that a closing account does not name is unconfirmed, not disproved.

09

China’s 2024 plan listed boarding

Link

Before the 2024 edition, a Xinhua report published on China’s defence ministry website said the exercise was held under the annual plan and by mutual consensus. It listed a joint surface strike, replenishment-at-sea positioning, joint search and rescue, and visit, board, search and seizure among the planned sea serials.

Qualification. This is a pre-exercise plan. It does not say which navy would conduct each serial; Singapore’s account of its ship’s role follows.

Activities and participation

Each edition paired one or two ships from each navy with planning ashore and a set of basic sea serials. The two governments often describe the same serials in different words.

13

China called 2024 longer and deeper

Link

As the 2024 edition opened, a Xinhua report published on China’s defence ministry website said that, compared with the two earlier editions, it ran longer, had more serials and involved deeper exchange. It said the two navies were building the exercise series into a signature programme.

Qualification. This is one participant’s self-assessment. The longer window is consistent with MINDEF’s dates (five days in 2024, four in 2023), but duration and the number of serials do not by themselves measure depth.

2026: the same serials in two vocabularies

Link

15

Singapore has sent one or two ships to each edition

Link

Across the five editions Singapore sent one or two ships: two in 2015, 2023 and 2025, and one frigate in 2024 and 2026. The PLA Navy’s named contribution was one ship in 2015, two in 2023, 2024 and 2025, and one frigate in 2026. The second ship was a mine warfare vessel in every two-ship contribution except Singapore’s 2015 corvette, and in 2024 China also listed shipborne helicopters and a marine detachment. Announced windows ran from four days in 2023 to eight in 2025. The comparable counts establish that Singapore’s ship commitment has stayed at one or two vessels; they do not show a rise in either navy’s ships, although China’s 2024 pre-exercise report stated an aim of expanding the exercise’s scale. The listed serials vary by edition rather than growing steadily.

Qualification. Ship numbers are one measure. Capability mix, duration and serials changed from edition to edition, personnel numbers are not consistently published, and no reviewed source measures the complexity of a serial. Durations are announced windows. A steady ship count shows neither stagnation nor deepening.

What the record establishes

Read together, the publications support a bounded but substantive conclusion.

32

Regular and institutionalised, with modest demonstrated depth

Link

China–Singapore naval cooperation has become regular. After a single edition in 2015 and an eight-year gap, Maritime Cooperation has been held every year since 2023, with the host alternating between Singapore and Zhanjiang. Singapore reports a pandemic pause in flagship exercises, which fits the absence of the edition planned for 2020; nothing reviewed explains 2016–2019. What the two navies report having done is consistent and basic: one or two ships a side, a few days of planning and exchange ashore, and at sea gunnery in every edition, usually with communications and manoeuvring and search and rescue, and medical evacuation, mine-warfare transits and replenishment approaches in some years. From 2024 on, advance plans listed serials that no closing account reviewed names: a joint surface strike in Chinese plans for 2024, 2025 and 2026, and boarding serials in Chinese plans for 2024 and 2025. For 2025 and 2026 a Chinese plan can be set against a Chinese completion account; for 2024 only Singapore’s account was reviewed. That does not establish whether the serials took place. The cooperation rests on a 2008 defence agreement enhanced in 2019. Its exercise and ministerial-dialogue commitments are documented in use; the hotline stops, in the publications reviewed, at a 2023 memorandum to work towards a link; and no instrument was found for the visiting-forces or logistics arrangements. Ship numbers have stayed at one or two a side. Limited published evidence of outcomes is not evidence that the cooperation lacks depth. The record supports describing an established and regular naval exercise relationship, small in the ships committed and basic in its reported serials, framed by China as deepening and by Singapore as one of many naval partnerships.

Qualification. This is IPR’s synthesis of official publications. It describes what was published, not what was achieved, and would change with Chinese closing accounts of the 2024 edition, Singaporean accounts of 2025 or evidence of the agreement’s other provisions in use.

Scope and limitations

Research question. How is China–Singapore naval cooperation evolving, and what does the public record establish about its scope, depth, and limits?

Thirteen publications preserved in IPR’s record from July–October 2026 and seventeen linked official pages from 2015–2025. The preserved records are PLA Daily reports, China defence ministry statements, MINDEF releases and one Xinhua report carried by PLA Daily. The linked pages are eleven MINDEF releases, including a parliamentary reply and a factsheet; one article from MINDEF’s PIONEER magazine; four Chinese reports published on China’s defence ministry website; and one China Military Online report.

Outside this scope: Unpublished activity, media and think-tank commentary, coast guard and non-naval cooperation, photographs and any claim to an exhaustive survey of either government’s reporting.

Compare dated official accounts edition by edition. Event dates stay distinct from publication dates. Advance plans stay distinct from closing accounts: an activity counts as reported completed only when a closing or post-exercise account names it. Agreement provisions are followed from commitment to signed instrument to reported use. Chinese evidence is read in the original. Linked pages outside the record are cited by publisher, date and link only.

The preserved record covers July to October 2026. Earlier editions rely on linked official pages checked on October 26 and November 13, 2026 and not preserved by IPR. A fuller Chinese navy account of the 2023 edition was found only on outlets outside IPR’s registered publishers and is not cited. No paid model processing was used. Restricted source text is not reproduced.
