No. 25 · Week ending 14 November 2026

Four Ministers Pledge a Coordination Center in the Philippines. Balikatan Already Tested One

During Balikatan 2026, Japanese and Australian personnel worked alongside American and Philippine forces at a coordination center in Camp Aguinaldo, according to the Philippines’ former military chief, whose account now offers a concrete point of comparison for the four governments’ October pledge and the institutional structure still awaiting definition.

By Benjamin Yang and Peter Nguyen

The four-government pledge

When defense ministers from Australia, Japan, the Philippines and the United States pledged on Oct. 28 to establish a Combined Coordination Center (CCC) in the Philippines, their joint statement published Nov. 10 introduced a new four-country undertaking with a name already familiar from the latest Balikatan exercise: during Balikatan 2026, forces from several nations had already worked inside a center at Camp Aguinaldo in Quezon City, where Gen. Romeo Brawner Jr., then the Philippines’ military chief, said Japanese and Australian personnel took part. That exercise experience gives the pledge a practical precedent, although the precise relationship between the Camp Aguinaldo facility and the proposed center, along with its eventual location, routine responsibilities and institutional identity, awaits an explanation from the governments involved.

Alongside the center pledge, the statement also calls for synchronized defense investments for the Philippines, expanded military exercises and closer policy consultations, yet the Australian release and Japan’s Japanese-language version describe the proposed center in terms that leave its location, opening date and staffing arrangements for subsequent decisions. When Manila Bulletin sought additional detail, the Philippine defense ministry offered a similarly limited account, while Japan’s readout of the Oct. 28 meeting and Koizumi’s news conference devoted greater attention to policy consultations, supply-and-services arrangements and negotiations on a military information-security agreement with Manila. Set against the ministers’ November 2025 readout, the latest commitment to a four-country center is therefore the most conspicuous new institutional proposal in the published statements.

From a bilateral planning room to multinational exercises

Long before the latest ministerial statement, the center’s name had appeared in accounts of U.S.–Philippine military exercises: A U.S. military report described a Combined Coordination Center synchronizing the two countries’ separate task forces during Balikatan 24 in 2024, and later that November, U.S. Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin and Philippine Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jr. broke ground on a facility at Camp Aguinaldo, headquarters of the Armed Forces of the Philippines, as they signed a bilateral General Security of Military Information Agreement (GSOMIA). Explaining the purpose of the facility, the U.S. Defense Department said it would support joint planning and coordinated responses to real-time incidents, including contingencies in the South China Sea, extending the center’s proposed role beyond the activities of a single military exercise.

During Balikatan 25, U.S. military reporting described a “secure, joint operations node” at which American and Philippine planners synchronized their respective task forces while each country retained authority within its own command structure, an arrangement that allowed the two militaries to coordinate their activities through a common planning venue. By Balikatan 2026, that structure had become part of a wider multinational exercise, with Adm. Samuel Paparo, then commander of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, saying that participants had “embedded this structure, including the newly inaugurated Combined Coordination Center,” according to the command-sponsored Indo-Pacific Defense FORUM. In that progression from bilateral planning to the participation of additional national forces lies the immediate operational background to the ministers’ subsequent proposal.

During the 2026 exercise, the Philippine Daily Inquirer reported Brawner’s account of President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. observing a live-fire drill through the center alongside personnel from the Philippines, United States, Japan, Australia, France and Canada; in an edited interview published by FORUM in September, Brawner separately recalled “elements from Japan and Australia” working inside the facility. His remarks provide the clearest published description of those countries’ participation, although regular peacetime assignments and the specific information available to individual personnel remain matters for further clarification, particularly given that U.S. Indo-Pacific Command said the exercise’s common operating picture—a shared display of operational information—was available to eight nations.

Manila’s account of a center already operating

When the joint statement reached the public in November, Philippine accounts portrayed a coordination mechanism at varying stages of development: Manila Standard described the center as a planned initiative on Nov. 12, whereas Defense Secretary Teodoro had told reporters a day earlier, “There will be a coordination mechanism. Right now, it is already operating,” GMA News reported. Teodoro said the governments might disclose certain locations once the participating parties reached an agreement, and the Philippine Daily Inquirer noted that his remarks left the relationship to the earlier Balikatan facility open, suggesting that an exercise-era arrangement could already be functioning while the eventual four-country framework continued to take shape.

Looking beyond Camp Aguinaldo, USNI News described U.S.-funded combined coordination centers at several Philippine locations during Balikatan 2026, raising the possibility that the ministers’ proposal might draw on a network of existing exercise facilities, although the particular locations and their relationship to the new arrangement await identification. Alongside the center pledge, the four military chiefs also signed Terms of Reference for a Pacific Chiefs of Defense Cooperation Council, cited in the joint statement, which Manila Bulletin says address information sharing and cooperation on planning and logistics; the place of that council within the proposed CCC arrangements remains to be explained. In a related but geographically distinct proposal, Brawner’s proposed communications links to allied command centers in Japan and Australia would connect Philippine facilities with headquarters overseas.

The next test comes between exercises

Within the Camp Aguinaldo arrangement, coordination allowed separate national task forces to exchange plans and maintain a common operational picture while preserving their respective chains of command, a distinction worth carrying into any reading of the October statement and its pledge to create a coordination center. Although Paparo has spoken of future exercises directed “through coalition combined command centers,” the four ministers’ published commitment uses narrower institutional language; meanwhile, Japan and the Philippines have an agreement governing reciprocal military access, and Koizumi also discussed arrangements for exchanging supplies and services with partner forces. Those existing agreements facilitate the movement and support of military personnel, leaving the proposed center’s command relationships and decision-making authorities for its participating governments to establish.

As the governments consider how to turn exercise coordination into a standing arrangement, sharing classified information will depend on agreements negotiated between participating states: the U.S.–Philippine General Security of Military Information Agreement (GSOMIA) governs exchanges between Washington and Manila, while Japan and the Philippines were working toward a comparable agreement in October, Koizumi said. During Balikatan 2026, a U.S.-provided common operating picture relied on foreign-disclosure officers and data tagging to determine how information moved across national networks, demonstrating an exercise-specific procedure while leaving the access available to individual Japanese and Australian officers, and the rules of any eventual four-country classified-information framework, for participating governments to establish.

Once the troops and exercise staffs have dispersed, the center’s purpose becomes a question of routine activity and actual incidents, especially because the 2024 U.S. fact sheet envisaged coordination during real-time contingencies as well as joint planning. GMA News separately reported that military-exercise coordination within the Armed Forces of the Philippines had moved from its Education and Training Command to Strategic Command, an organizational shift that offers some context for Manila’s account of an operating mechanism; nevertheless, the new four-country statement leaves peacetime billets, incident responsibilities and long-term financing to future arrangements. Although USNI News identified U.S. funding for facilities used during Balikatan, the financial arrangements for the proposed four-country initiative have yet to be publicly specified.

At Balikatan, Japanese and Australian personnel worked alongside their American and Philippine counterparts in a coordination center, according to the Philippines’ former military chief, establishing an exercise precedent for the multinational initiative now under discussion. Turning that experience into a standing arrangement would require the governments to determine which officers would serve there, what information each could receive and how the center would respond during maritime incidents, while the ministers have pledged a CCC whose ultimate foundation could be Camp Aguinaldo, a network of facilities or a distinct organization. The eventual choices on staffing, information access and routine responsibilities will establish how far the exercise-era arrangements develop into sustained four-country cooperation.
