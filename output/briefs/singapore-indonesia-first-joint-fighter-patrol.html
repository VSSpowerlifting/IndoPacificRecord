Singapore and Indonesia Fly Their First Joint Fighter Patrol

Their first coordinated fighter patrol near the Singapore Strait puts years of defence planning into practice; its lasting value will depend on what happens after the inaugural flight.

By Dylan Tanzi

The first coordinated patrol

For more than four decades, fighter crews from Singapore and Indonesia have learned to fly alongside one another in joint exercises. On November 4, they took that familiarity into a different setting. Four F-16s, two from each air force, conducted the inaugural Patkor Indopura patrol along the airspace bordering the two countries, near the Singapore Strait. Singapore’s air force chief, Major-General Kelvin Fan, flew in a Singaporean fighter, while Indonesia’s Air Chief Marshal Mohamad Tonny Harjono joined the mission in an Indonesian jet. Their presence gave the flight an unusually prominent beginning to what both governments hope will be closer cooperation; the more consequential change was that the aircraft were carrying out a border-security assignment rather than an exercise scenario.

The operation ran from November 3 to 4, according to Singapore’s Ministry of Defence, and began with combined briefings and the rehearsal of standing operating procedures before the fighters flew their route. Indonesia’s own account, published a day later, identifies the F-16 carrying Harjono as belonging to its 16th Air Squadron and places the flight near the Strait. Those details offer an early view of the cooperation behind the photographs. Crews from separate forces had to prepare for the same mission and work to shared procedures in a real border area, an activity that draws on habits developed during training but puts them to a practical use. The two releases do not describe how responsibilities were divided once the aircraft were airborne.

The governments describe the patrol through somewhat different security priorities. Singapore framed it as part of protecting the border against transnational terrorism, while Indonesia emphasized airspace security and regional stability near the Singapore Strait, a heavily used international shipping corridor. These accounts are compatible, although neither documents a particular threat encountered during the mission. Their contrast helps explain the appeal of a coordinated patrol. The airspace matters to both countries, but the reasons each government foregrounds need not be identical for their air forces to cooperate. What Patkor Indopura creates, at least in its first outing, is a setting in which those overlapping interests can be translated into a joint peacetime task.

From a defence agreement to a patrol

That task emerged from a longer sequence of political and military decisions. The bilateral Defence Cooperation Agreement entered into force in March 2024, giving the two governments a framework for expanding practical defence activities. In March 2025, Singapore’s then defence minister, Ng Eng Hen, told parliament that the first Patkor Indopura patrol would take place later that year. By July, the project had reached the Defence Cooperation Committee, the senior bilateral body charged with overseeing the agreement’s implementation. During its second meeting, officials publicly listed an inaugural fighter patrol among the initiatives being prepared. Seen against that sequence, the November sortie was an implementation step in a relationship already being developed, rather than a sudden reaction to a newly reported security incident.

The timetable adds another dimension to the story. Singapore had publicly expected the inaugural patrol in 2025; the first documented operation took place on November 4, 2026. The official accounts leave the reason for that later date unexplained. Even so, the sequence tells us something about the nature of the initiative. Announcing bilateral cooperation is a political act, whereas putting pilots through joint preparations and sending them along a border requires organizations on both sides to translate that promise into a mission. The first patrol is evidence that this particular commitment reached that second stage, although a single sortie cannot tell us whether it has become a durable routine.

In August 2025, Defence Minister Chan Chun Sing stood before an audience at Indonesia’s National Resilience Institute and recalled his years serving in the country. He told them that many in his generation had grown up alongside their Indonesian counterparts, drawing a line from personal friendships to decades of military exchanges. In the same speech, he placed the forthcoming air patrol beside a coordinated naval patrol, CORPAT INDOSIN, launched in 2023. The navies had already taken cooperation into a peacetime security setting; Patkor Indopura suggested an analogous ambition for the air forces. Airspace and maritime patrols involve different practical demands, but both can turn habits developed during exercises into work performed in a shared security environment.

The work after the inaugural flight

For the pilots, the deeper history runs through Exercise Elang Indopura, which began in 1980. At the May 2025 edition, more than 100 personnel took part at Roesmin Nurjadin Air Force Base in Indonesia. Singapore deployed four F-16C/D fighters and an A330 Multi-Role Tanker Transport aircraft; Indonesia contributed two F-16s. Together the crews practised air-to-air refuelling and air-to-ground training at the Siabu Air Weapons Range. Such exercises build the familiarity and technical proficiency needed when people from different forces operate in proximity. They also take place within a planned training framework, where participants know the objectives and can prepare for the scenarios in advance.

A border patrol introduces another layer. The purpose is to carry out a peacetime security mission along an area both governments have a stake in protecting, not simply to demonstrate that their aircraft can refuel or manoeuvre together. Singapore’s report says the two air forces exercised common procedures and patrolled together, which is an important beginning. For that arrangement to become useful beyond the inaugural flight, however, commanders and crews would need a dependable way to coordinate responsibilities when circumstances depart from the plan. The published accounts leave the mission’s communications arrangements, decision-making boundaries and handling of unexpected situations largely undescribed. For now, the public record establishes the patrol and its shared preparations; the depth of operational coordination remains to be demonstrated.

Putting both air force chiefs in the cockpit gave the debut unmistakable political weight; making the patrol last will fall to the aircrews who come after them. The next published missions will show whether the shared briefings and procedures of November have become something repeatable, carried out without senior commanders aboard. If they do, Patkor Indopura will have achieved more than an inaugural flight: it will have turned a long relationship built through training into a dependable piece of peacetime border security.
